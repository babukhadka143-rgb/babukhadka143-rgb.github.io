After the Flood, What Should Farmers Plant?

Rethinking Agricultural Recovery Through Crop Diversity in Nepal

By Pradip Babu Khadka · Agriculture & Forestry University

This first website version contains the article's central framing and structure. The complete final assignment text can be pasted into this article section before submission.

Recovery should mean more than replacement

When floods destroy standing crops, the immediate priority is understandably to restore production. But recovery also creates a question about what farmers should plant next. Replacing one damaged crop with exactly the same crop may restore the previous system without addressing the vulnerability that made the farming system fragile in the first place.

The August 2026 Bhote Koshi–Trishuli floods provide a useful case for thinking about this issue in Nepal. Agricultural recovery after such events can be viewed not only as reconstruction, but also as an opportunity to rethink crop and varietal diversity, farmer profitability, seed access, markets and climate resilience.

Why crop diversity matters

Crop and varietal diversity can spread risk across different crops, maturity periods, growing conditions and market opportunities. However, diversity is not automatically beneficial simply because more crops are grown. The selected crops and varieties need to fit local soils, climate, water conditions, labour availability, household needs and market demand.

Technology alone does not travel

A technically suitable variety becomes a practical resilience tool only when farmers can actually access its seed and understand how to use it. Seed systems, extension services and farmer-to-farmer networks therefore matter alongside agricultural technology. A resilient recovery plan needs to connect varieties with the people, institutions and markets that can make them work.

Recovery needs a whole system

For post-flood agricultural planning, crop choice should be considered together with profitability, agronomic suitability, seed systems, markets and damaged infrastructure. Crop diversity can contribute to resilience, but only when it is economically realistic and supported by functioning systems.

What should farmers plant after a flood? The answer should not be determined by a single crop or a single technology. It should emerge from local conditions, farmer priorities and a diversified recovery strategy that reduces future vulnerability while protecting livelihoods.
